SyncMapRGCI · research presentation

RG: Recursive Grouping

Hierarchical chunking inside one self-organizing map —
from SyncMap to recursive multi-scale dynamics

Laboratory of Intelligent Systems, Kyushu University · October 2026
Code, data and every result in this deck: github.com/Cyletix/SyncMapRGCI

This talk answers three questions. What exactly changed relative to the original SyncMap? How does information move between scales in RG? And what do controlled experiments say about where RG helps, where it does not, and what it costs? Every number shown comes from the public repository and can be reproduced with the commands in the appendix.
In one sentence

The same self-organizing kernel, run again on the tokens it produces

  • SyncMap organizes symbols in one space; DBSCAN reads clusters afterwards.
  • RG turns stable groups into tokens and feeds them to the same kernel one level up, at a 3.16× larger scale and slower clock.
  • Lower levels make unsupervised tokens; upper levels learn slower, coarser relations.
  • Scores still come from one common readout of the level-0 map, so RG gets no evaluation shortcut.
RG is not "SyncMap followed by a hierarchical clustering step". The coarse-grained tokens become a new input sequence, so higher levels have their own relation statistics and their own dynamics. At the same time the evaluation never reads RG's internal tokens as the answer: all five models are scored by the same reader applied to their final level-0 coordinates.

PART 1

The problem

Find the chunks hidden in a symbol stream — and the chunks of chunks — without labels.

Task

Recover hidden chunks, and their nesting, from a random walk

A random walk over a .dot graph emits one symbol at a time. Each model sees only that stream and must place symbols so that true chunks (flat) or every nested level (hierarchy) come out as clusters. Labels are used only for scoring.

This is the whole task in one picture. On the left is the generator, DCH1: 18 symbols, 6 chunks of three, and 2 groups of three chunks. The walk mostly stays inside a chunk, sometimes moves to a sibling chunk, and rarely crosses to the other group. On the right is RG's learned map after 100,000 steps from the stream alone: the two groups are far apart, and inside each group the chunks form smaller clusters.
Starting point

Original SyncMap: co-active symbols contract, then DBSCAN reads clusters

  1. Every symbol is a point in a Euclidean space.
  2. The positive set PSt (recently active) contracts to its centre cp.
  3. The negative set NSt contracts to cn.
  4. After training, DBSCAN turns coordinates into a partition: points connected by dense neighbourhoods share an ID; isolated points may be noise (−1).

Vargas & Asabuki, AAAI 2021. The repository's syncmap baseline ports the reference release with a constant learning rate.

This is the version the audience already knows. Training only moves points; the discrete partition comes afterwards from DBSCAN, which needs no labels. RG keeps this split between dynamics and readout, but changes almost everything on the dynamics side.
Why it is hard

Two hard cases: transitive chunks and nested levels

Both maps are the real P(next | current) of the DOT files; the lines are the true boundaries, drawn only for the viewer.

Left: in chain_mixed5_6 some chunks are dense cliques but others are paths. In a path chunk, two members far apart in the chain never co-occur in one step; their membership only follows from multi-step reachability. Right: in DCH1 the same leaf transitions carry two scales at once, chunks of three inside groups of nine. A single distance scale has to represent both a small within-chunk spacing and a large between-group spacing.
Motivation

One geometry, one scale: what goes wrong on a hierarchy

TSFMap spreads the six chunks evenly over its normalised sphere: the fine level is perfect (L1 ARI 0.97), but the two groups are not separated (L2 0.42).

SyncMap, Symmetric and NSGD do the opposite: two far-apart blobs (L2 = 1.00) with the chunks inside merged (L1 0.26–0.33). RG keeps both (L1 0.59, L2 1.00).

These are the models' own 3-D coordinates, not a projection, after 100,000 steps on seed 0. The failure mode is visible only in 3-D: TSFMap normalises every step, so its chunks end up evenly spaced on a sphere with no group-level structure. The other single-geometry models show the opposite trade-off. Per-level numbers are five-seed means from the benchmark. RG's design goal is exactly to hold two well-separated scales at once.
PART 2

From SyncMap to RG

Lineage, the level kernel, and the five changes that make recursion work.

Lineage

After Early NS the work split into two branches

RG did not start from scratch. Early NS tried a second-order spring-mass system with an explicit relation graph to fix SyncMap's mutual locking, instability on small graphs and lack of relation memory. Inertia caused overshoot and the system kept accumulating patches. Two branches followed: NSGD, a first-order system with plasticity and an active pulse, and the current SyncMapNS, a rewritten kernel designed to be reused at every level of RG. They share the lessons of Early NS, not a class or a parameter preset, so this is not a serial version chain.
Prerequisite

NSGD: make the dynamics first-order before making them hierarchical

Early NS · second order

m v̇ = F − γ v,   ẋ = v
  • position + velocity + damping
  • inertia → overshoot and ringing
  • patches added on top of the relation springs

NSGD · first order

Δx = η · P · F
  • positions move directly along the net force
  • no velocity, no inertia
  • online relation graph, plasticity P and an active pulse
This step fixed the optimiser skeleton, not the hierarchy. NSGD is kept as a strong baseline in every experiment.
NSGD keeps an online relation graph and moves positions directly; plasticity and the active-centre pulse are its adaptation mechanisms. It should not be read as having a convergence guarantee: the relation graph itself changes over time.
Level kernel

SyncMapNS: one reusable level; RG adds recursion outside it

  • Shared with NSGD: first-order updates, coordinates driven by an online relation graph.
  • Rewritten: transition statistics, a bidirectional spring with a rest length, degree normalisation.
  • RG's kernel switches off NSGD's plasticity and pulse and keeps the primitive force and the relation spring.
  • Token extraction, coarse-graining and the multi-level schedule live in SyncMapRG, not in the kernel.
SyncMapNS is not an alias, subclass or preset of NSGD; they are independent runtimes. What matters for hierarchy is the interface: every level runs the same dynamics and levels differ only in spatial scale and in the time window of their evidence. Later we switch recursion off to see what the outer RG layer contributes on its own.
Input

One random walk feeds two signals: an immediate force and a relation memory

The environment holds each state for about eleven input steps, time_delay = 10, and emits the current state together with an exponentially decaying tail of the previous one. So around a switch from A to B the frames look like {A}, {A, B}, {B}. That overlap is the only place where transition evidence lives. The primitive force reads the observation directly. The tracker thresholds it at 0.1 into an active set and pairs consecutive active sets. The original SyncMap has no relation buffer at all; it relies on the same previous-state tail.
Change 1

Adjacent observations become a persistent, undirected relation graph

Counts decay with memory M = 300 observations; every Tpush = 100 steps the strongest neighbours (top-k = 12, above threshold) become spring edges.

RG does not use the benchmark's true edges. It remembers which symbols keep appearing in consecutive thresholded observations. Self pairs are skipped, pairs are written symmetrically, so the graph is undirected and carries no causal direction. Similarity is the decayed pair count divided by the geometric mean of the two symbols' decayed activity, a cosine-style normalisation shared with NSGD. Cost note: the tracker keeps a dense N-by-N table, so doubling the symbols roughly quadruples this part of the work; top-k only sparsifies the final edges.
Change 2

Keep SyncMap's contrastive force, made explicit and bounded

For the current active set Pt with weighted centre ct:

Fi+ = a (ct − zi) / max(‖ct − zi‖, dmin),  i ∈ Pt
Fj− = b r0 (zj − ct) / max(‖zj − ct‖, dmin)2,  j ∉ Pt

a = 0.5, b = 2.0. The push falls off as 1/distance (a vector divided by the squared distance); a distance floor and a per-step cap keep it bounded. Label-free, read straight from the input.

This is SyncMap's original idea in a cleaner form: active points move toward their common centre, inactive points move away from it with a push that falls off as one over the distance. It does not go through the relation graph and never uses labels. It works together with the relation spring; the experiments here do not separate the two forces' individual contributions.
Change 3

A relation edge has a preferred length — it does not only pull

  • Every edge has a target distance rL (the level's natural length).
  • Too far → attraction; too close → strongly non-linear repulsion.
  • The same normalised curve is reused at every level; edge weight and node degree only scale its strength.
  • kattr = 0.07 keeps the spring a secondary force.
This canonical spring is zero exactly at the rest length, pushes hard when compressed and pulls back when stretched, so relation edges cannot squeeze all points into one spot, which was SyncMap's mutual-locking problem. The plot is the normalised factor for one ordinary edge, not the net displacement after weights, degree normalisation, cross-macro attenuation, other forces, learning rate and clipping. The spring constant 0.07 is a working point, not a swept optimum.
Two separations

Two ways points are pushed apart: per observation, and per macro group

Left: only the primitive contrastive component. The two active points move toward their weighted centre, so they approach each other; the grey inactive point is pushed along inactive-minus-centre. This component does not push back on the active points. The full micro force also contains the spring and a local obstacle term that does act on active endpoints. Right: the macro boundary, which needs the tokenization introduced later. When the nearest members of two macro groups are closer than the target spacing, the same bounded shift is applied to every member, so the arrows start at the centroids; the gauge below shows the nearest-point gap that triggers it.
Level 0

The level-0 update: sum the micro forces, clip, step

ΔZmicro = η · clip( Fprimitive + Fspring + Fobstacle )

primitive

immediate contrast from the current observation

spring

relation-graph edges with rest length rL

obstacle

endpoint-local radial repulsion, krepulse = 1

  • First-order: no mass, no velocity. η = 0.05, net step capped at 2.0.
  • The macro group shift (later) is a separate bounded displacement, not part of this clip.
Before any upper-level feedback exists, this is the whole level-0 update: three force terms are added, clipped together, and multiplied by the learning rate. Geometric patch forces from earlier experiments, such as entity boundaries, sibling attraction or line obstacles, are switched off in the maintained configuration.
Change 4

Tokenize, keep the membership, and keep learning one level up

The key is not to cluster once, but to keep the membership and turn each set into a new letter. Stable groups at level 0 become tokens; the token stream is the input of level 1, which runs the same kernel with its own coordinates and its own relation statistics, at a slower and coarser scale. The figure repeats the level-0 points only to show nesting; the real upper levels do not copy level-0 coordinates.
Scales

Why the scale factor is 3.16

  • r0 = 1 is the normalised spring length at level 0, not a measured constant.
  • 3.16 ≈ √10: a half-decade, log-even ladder 1 → 3.16 → 10 → 31.6 → 100.
  • 3.16 > 2 clears the straightened span of a two-hop chain of unit edges, leaving room for the coarse scale.
  • A motivated choice, not a theorem; no sweep shows it is the unique optimum.
The space ladder inserts one stable logarithmic step between decades, and the first coarse scale exceeds the span of a two-hop local chain. Relation memory grows ten-fold per level because higher levels must integrate slower evidence. Earlier write-ups called 3.16 an experimentally determined separation ratio; the evidence for that was too weak, so I no longer claim it.
Known limitation

Level cadence: the default scheduler aliases deep clocks

Higher levels should integrate longer windows, so the target interface cadence grows by 3.16 per level: 50, 158, 499 raw steps. A code audit found that the default scheduler checks every level against the same raw-step modulo, and a deeper level only gets the chance to check when the level below fires. The 158-step target therefore aliases to lcm(50, 158) = 3,950, and the next level to about two million. This is a confirmed scheduling flaw, not an inference from scores. An elapsed-time option exists but it also changes event counts, repeated force exposure and graph-rebuild phase, so it is not a clean A/B and is not validated. The release keeps the default and states the flaw. The practical consequence: within 100,000 steps, levels 2 and above receive very little evidence.
Change 5

Macro feedback keeps coarse groups separable inside level 0

This is the second separation mechanism. RG forms stable tokens and macro labels from its own geometry and statistics; they are not true labels. The feedback does two bounded things in level 0: the attractive part of springs that cross a macro boundary is scaled by 0.001, while their compressive repulsion is untouched; and when the nearest members of two macro groups come closer than the target spacing, both groups are translated apart as wholes. Upper-level coordinates are never broadcast down rigidly.
Readout

From coordinates to partitions: one reader for every model

This step turns each model's continuous map into partitions that can be scored. Flat graphs use DBSCAN. Hierarchies use the final level-0 coordinates: build the Euclidean minimum spanning tree, sort its N minus 1 edges, and cut where the ratio between neighbouring edge lengths is at least 1.05, giving one cut per required level. It is a single-checkpoint rule; the supplementary runs therefore also average the last five checkpoints. The right side is a research candidate I have not adopted: compare the two edge-length regimes on each side of a cut, require a geometric margin and persistence over a late window, and keep several nested cuts. RG's internal tokens influence training but are never read as the answer.
Summary

If the audience only knows the original SyncMap, these five rows are the shortest answer to "what did you change".
PART 3

How we score

Metrics, the hierarchy score, the benchmark, and what "equal" means.

Metric

ARI checks the whole partition; NMI measures shared information

  • NMI: how much information predicted and true labels share.
  • ARI: every pair of symbols, same-or-different cluster, corrected for chance. 1 = identical, ≈0 = random, can be negative.
  • Over-splitting keeps a lot of information, so NMI stays high while ARI drops.
  • All rankings use ARI; NMI is reported as a diagnostic.
The question here is whether the predicted partition really matches the target, not only whether it carries information about it. NMI is more forgiving of over-splitting, many clusters and noise handling, so we rank with ARI. All values on the left are computed directly with sklearn.
Hierarchy score

Hierarchies: average ARI over every required level

ARIhier = (1/D) ΣL=1…D ARIL
  • Every required level counts equally; a missing level scores 0.
  • Per-level ARI is always reported next to it, so the weakest level stays visible.
  • Level 1 is the finest; the top level of the benchmark graphs has only two groups.
An earlier "joint minimum" rule let the single worst cut decide the whole score and gave no credit for partial success; it is not used here.
Which hierarchy metric to use depends on the ground truth. Here every graph has explicit labels for every level, so I score each level with ARI and average. A worst-level view is kept by reporting all levels. Tree-edit distance is not used because the output is a set of nested partitions, not a tree with comparable node semantics.

Equal steps are not equal compute

3-D maps · flat

Imbalanced communities: RG keeps the large and the small chunks apart

imbalanced_fixed_300 · 300 states in 14 chunks of very different sizes · seed 0 · each model in its own 3-D space.

This is RG's clearest flat result in the benchmark: 0.51 against 0.37 for the next model. Very unequal chunk sizes are hard for one density threshold. A plausible reading, not tested here, is that the rest-length spring spreads large chunks without letting them swallow small ones.
Over time

Scores jump between checkpoints — endpoints alone are noisy

IEH, seed 0, ARI every 2,000 steps. The benchmark table uses the final checkpoint; the supplementary experiments average the last five checkpoints (last 10,000 steps) instead.

The hierarchy readout is a discrete cut of a moving map, so it can jump by 0.2 or more between neighbouring checkpoints. That is one reason five seeds are not enough to separate close models, and why the supplementary runs report a late-window mean.
PART 5

Targeted experiments

Where does each model win, and does RG's hierarchy help when the coarse level is hard?

Design

Three questions the benchmark could not answer

A · Orientation

Is RG better on few large communities and NSGD on many small ones? Seven 300-state graphs from 3×100 to 60×5.

B · Harder hierarchies

Does RG keep coarse groups apart when they are weakly separated? Four hierarchies × three mixing levels.

C · Recursion

Is it the recursion that helps? RG with level1_enabled=false: same kernel and readout, no tokens, no upper levels, no macro feedback.

  • 100k steps, 5 paired seeds (one seed = the same walk for every model), all five models plus RG without recursion: 535 runs.
  • Score: formal ARI averaged over the last 5 checkpoints (last 10k steps), not the final one.
  • Diagnostic: oracle-K — the same map cut at the true cluster count — separates "the map lacks it" from "the readout misses it".
  • Every state is visited within 0.4k–34k steps on every graph, so 100k steps is at least 3× full coverage.
These experiments were designed after looking at the benchmark: its flat graphs only hint at where each model wins, and its hierarchies are too easy at the coarse level to test RG's multi-scale design. Everything else is kept fixed: the same profiles, the same readout and the same 100,000 steps. Two changes make the numbers steadier: the score is a late-window average, and seeds are paired, so we can count on how many seeds one model beats another.
A · Orientation

Community orientation decides the flat winner

  • RG beats NSGD on every seed from 3×100 to 15×20, by 0.23–0.38; at 20×15 by 0.09.
  • NSGD wins from 30×10 (by 0.29) and is perfect on 60×5, where RG scores 0.37.
  • Crossover: between 20 and 30 communities of 15–10 states.
  • Nobody solves few large chain-shaped communities: RG's best is 0.60 (10×30). TSFMap fails throughout.
All seven graphs have exactly 300 states; only the split into communities changes. NSGD improves steadily with the number of communities, consistent with a strong relation spring plus an active pulse that stabilises many small, dense neighbourhoods quickly. RG peaks at ten communities of thirty and degrades as communities shrink. The two curves cross between twenty and thirty communities. These are paired comparisons over five seeds; for example at 5×60 RG is ahead on all five seeds with p below 0.001.

B · Results

When the coarse level gets hard, NSGD collapses and RG holds

At the hardest mixing NSGD drops to 0.06–0.10 on HIMB3, H4x4x5 and H3x3x3x4; RG keeps 0.42, 0.33 and 0.17 (5/5 seeds each). RG leads on DCH1 at easy and medium mixing.

At easy and medium mixing they tie on HIMB3 and H4x4x5, and NSGD is clearly ahead on the three-level H3x3x3x4 (0.62 vs 0.38, 0/5 seeds for RG).

Level-mean ARI, late-window average, five seeds, error bars one standard deviation. Two messages. First, a hard coarse level is where RG's design pays off: NSGD loses the top-level groups while RG keeps them. Second, RG is not uniformly better: on the deepest graph with clearly separated levels NSGD wins the formal score. The next two slides ask how much of that is the readout and how much is the recursion. SyncMap, not shown here, is surprisingly robust at the hardest mixing (0.43 on HIMB3, 0.36 on H3x3x3x4); Symmetric and TSFMap collapse on the new graphs; all numbers are in results/supp/REPORT.md.

It leaks the true count, so it is never a score, but it answers a different question: is the structure in the map? Final checkpoint, five-seed means; the full table is in the supplementary report. Two consequences. The single-checkpoint MST readout is now the main bottleneck for both models, and improving it is the most valuable next step. And RG's large formal advantages at high mixing are real advantages of the map, not artefacts of the readout.

PART 6

Conclusions

What the evidence supports, what it does not, and what comes next.

Supported

What the evidence supports

  • RG and NSGD are complementary: community orientation decides flat graphs (crossover at 20–30 communities of 300 states).
  • On hierarchies with weakly separated coarse levels RG keeps the groups apart where NSGD collapses — and the map, not only the score, shows it.
  • The recursion causes the gain: removing it costs 0.05–0.31 level-mean ARI in every hierarchy setting, more as mixing grows.
  • On the small benchmark hierarchies RG's advantage is at the fine level; their coarse level is solved by four of five models.
These are system-level results at 100,000 equal input steps with the maintained profiles; the recursion ablation is the only component-level test.
Each bullet maps to a specific experiment: orientation sweep, hierarchy difficulty sweep with oracle-K, the recursion ablation, and the benchmark's per-level table.
Not supported · limitations

What it does not show

  • No universal winner. NSGD wins many small communities and the three-level graph at easy mixing; at the hardest mixing SyncMap matches or beats RG on two of the four hierarchies.
  • No compute claim. RG costs 3–4× NSGD on flat graphs and 10–20× on hierarchies per step; nothing here is equal wall-clock.
  • The readout is a bottleneck. The single-checkpoint MST cut loses 0.07–0.40 ARI for every model and decides some comparisons.
  • Deep levels are under-fed. The default scheduler aliases upper clocks (50 → 3,950 → ~2M steps); the deepest results are limited by that, not only by the dynamics.
  • Variance. RG's seed spread is often the largest; five seeds separate large effects, not small ones.
  • Scope. Synthetic graph walks only, 100,000 steps, 3-D maps; no visual data and no causal inference yet.
These limits do not overturn the large structural effects, but they bound how the small differences may be stated. In particular, "RG is better" without a qualifier is never correct for these results.
Next

What comes next

1 · Readout

A persistent, multi-checkpoint cut rule (the candidate protocol in Part 2), validated against the oracle-K gap.

2 · Scheduler

Give upper levels their intended cadence, then repeat the deep-hierarchy experiments.

3 · Fair budgets

Equal wall-clock and matched memory alongside equal steps; ablate NSGD's spring and pulse as RG's recursion was ablated.

4 · Causal inference

Directed relations between the chunks RG finds: the CI line, from directed transition statistics to a tested causal-structure method.

The first two items are now the most valuable, because the experiments show that both the readout and the deep-level schedule limit what RG's maps can express in the score. The fourth item is the original research goal: hierarchical chunks are the units between which causal relations should be inferred.
Take-aways

Three sentences

  1. RG turns SyncMap from one geometry into recursive multi-scale dynamics: unsupervised tokens become the next level's input.
  2. The recursion measurably helps where coarse structure is weak, and RG wins few-to-medium communities, while NSGD wins many small ones.
  3. It is not a universal winner: gains, cost, variance and the readout all depend on the structure.

Everything in this deck: github.com/Cyletix/SyncMapRGCI · benchmark report · supplementary report · this presentation in docs/presentation/

If the audience remembers one thing: RG's contribution is not "another clustering step", it is that unsupervised coarse-graining becomes the next level's input and keeps acting on the dynamics. The experiments show that this bias fits some structures and clearly exposes its costs; the readout and the level clock are the open problems.
